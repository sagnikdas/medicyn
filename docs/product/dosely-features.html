What is offline-first reliability?

The reminders themselves don't depend on an internet connection to fire. Scheduling, storage, and alert-triggering all happen locally on the device — once a medication is set up (photographed, dosage confirmed), the phone's own OS-level notification system handles firing the reminder at the right time, with no server round-trip needed at that moment.

This matters in a few concrete scenarios:

  • Rural or low-connectivity homes — elderly users often live with spotty WiFi/cellular, or turn off mobile data to save money.
  • Airplane mode or dead-zone gaps — a dropped connection at exactly 8am shouldn't mean the dose reminder silently fails to fire.
  • India-market relevance — connectivity reliability is a bigger day-to-day issue there than in the US, making this a stronger differentiator in that market specifically.

It's also a trust argument for a life-safety-adjacent feature: a medication reminder that occasionally fails because of a network hiccup is worse than useless — it creates false confidence the system is watching when it isn't. No researched competitor leads with this claim explicitly. Caveat: this covers the reminder-firing mechanic, not the caregiver dashboard/family-sharing sync — relaying a missed-dose alert to someone elsewhere still needs connectivity, so marketing copy should be precise about which piece is offline-guaranteed.

What is pharmacy-agnostic tracking?

Dosely can track and manage medications no matter which pharmacy filled the prescription — CVS, Walgreens, an independent local pharmacy, a mail-order service, or one in another country entirely. The app isn't tied to, or dependent on, any single pharmacy's systems, inventory, or purchase history.

This matters because of how the retail competitors are actually built: Amazon Pharmacy (PillPack), CVS's MedRemind, and Walgreens' in-app reminders are all "free" — but only because they're bundled into that specific retailer's own pharmacy service. They can only remind you about medications they filled and sold you. If a family fills one prescription at CVS and another at an independent local pharmacy, none of those apps give a unified view — each retailer's app only knows about its own slice.

Because dosely captures medications via photo of the label rather than pulling from a pharmacy's order system, it works identically regardless of where the prescription came from. A caregiver managing an elderly parent's medications — split across two or three pharmacies plus over-the-counter supplements — gets one complete list instead of three fragmented apps. This turns the retail giants' biggest structural advantage (owning the pharmacy) into their biggest limitation (only useful for their own pharmacy).

What is photo-label + voice dosage AI confirmation?

Dosely's core capture workflow — four steps, replacing manual form-filling entirely:

  • Photograph the label — point the camera at the prescription bottle; OCR/AI reads the medication name, strength, and instructions off the label instead of the user typing them in.
  • Say the dosage out loud — the user or caregiver speaks the actual dosage being taken (e.g. "one tablet, twice a day"), capturing real-world usage even when it doesn't exactly match the printed label.
  • Review what the AI understood — the app shows back its interpretation of both the label and the spoken dosage, so it can be corrected before it becomes a reminder.
  • Confirm — done — the reminder schedule is set automatically, no manual form-filling.

Every other reminder app in the comp set — Medisafe, MyTherapy, Dosecast, even hardware players like Hero — requires manually typing the medication name, dosage, and schedule into a form. None combine AI label-reading with voice-based dosage confirmation in one flow. This removes the single biggest friction point (manual data entry) in every competing app, which matters most for elderly users who struggle with small-form-factor typing. It's also why reminders can fire with "zero signal" — the capture-and-confirm loop happens on-device at setup, so the resulting reminder doesn't depend on a server round-trip later.

What is family/caregiver sharing + missed-dose alerts?

Lets more than one person have visibility into a medication schedule — typically the elderly person taking the medication, plus one or more family members or caregivers who aren't in the same house. When a dose is confirmed as taken, everyone with access sees it. When a scheduled dose passes without confirmation, the system escalates: instead of just re-notifying the person who might not respond, it alerts a designated caregiver directly.

How it typically works:

  • Invite/link a caregiver — the primary user (or a caregiver setting things up on their behalf) adds family members to the medication circle.
  • Shared visibility — caregivers see the same medication list, schedule, and adherence history as the primary user, without needing to ask.
  • Missed-dose escalation — if a dose isn't confirmed within a set window (e.g. 30–60 minutes after the scheduled time), a push notification goes to the linked caregiver(s), not just a repeat alert to the person who already missed it.
  • Multiple caregivers per person, or one caregiver across multiple people — e.g. siblings splitting responsibility for one parent, or one adult child managing both aging parents from a single account.

This is the feature Medisafe calls "Medfriend," called out in the research as a proven driver of premium upgrades — people will pay for the peace of mind of knowing rather than hoping a parent took their medication. It's also the exact feature CareZone was flagged as lacking even at $150M in funding and 3.5M users. Building this well is a real opportunity to outdo the one funded example that already tried and reportedly under-delivered on it.

What are drug-drug interaction warnings?

Safety alerts that fire when two or more medications a person is taking can negatively affect each other — either reducing effectiveness, dangerously amplifying an effect, or causing a side effect neither drug causes on its own.

Some concrete examples:

  • Warfarin (blood thinner) + aspirin — both increase bleeding risk; combined, the risk compounds.
  • Statins (cholesterol) + certain antibiotics (e.g. clarithromycin) — can raise statin levels enough to cause muscle damage.
  • ACE inhibitors (blood pressure) + potassium supplements — can push potassium to dangerous levels, risking heart arrhythmia.
  • Sedatives/opioids + alcohol or other sedatives — compounding effect can suppress breathing.

How it would work in dosely: every time a new medication is added (via the photo-label capture), the app cross-references it against everything already on the person's list using a drug-interaction database — RxNorm (NIH/NLM, free, standardizes drug names) paired with a proper interaction-checking API like DrugBank or First Databank/Medi-Span (commercial, more clinically complete). If a conflict is found, the app flags it — e.g. "⚠️ This may interact with your blood thinner — check with your pharmacist."

Elderly users are disproportionately on multiple medications at once ("polypharmacy," often 5+ drugs) — exactly when interaction risk climbs and exactly when a family caregiver managing the list from outside can't catch it manually. It's a trust/safety signal more than a feature. Build note: RxNorm is free and NIH-maintained, so the realistic path is integrating an existing interaction-checking API rather than building a clinical drug database in-house.

What is doctor-ready adherence export?

A one-tap (or automatic) export of someone's full medication history — what they've been prescribed, the schedule, and how consistently they've actually taken it — formatted as a clean report a doctor or pharmacist can review quickly, rather than the caregiver trying to recall or explain it verbally during a rushed appointment.

What it would typically include:

  • Full medication list — names, dosages, schedules, start dates.
  • Adherence rate over time — e.g. "94% of doses taken on schedule over the past 30 days," ideally broken down per medication.
  • Missed-dose pattern — not just a raw percentage, but when doses tend to get missed (e.g. consistently missing the evening dose) — diagnostically useful, since a doctor might adjust the schedule rather than just note non-adherence.
  • A shareable format — a PDF or printable view that can be handed over, emailed ahead of an appointment, or pulled up on a phone in the waiting room.

Low-cost, high-value: dosely already has every piece of this data as a byproduct of the core photo+voice confirmation loop — nothing new needs to be captured, it's purely a presentation/export layer. Both Medisafe and MyTherapy already ship this, so it's table stakes rather than a novel wedge, but cheap enough that there's no good reason to skip it. The value isn't really "a report" — it's removing a specific, recurring pain point: caregivers walking into a doctor's appointment and being asked "how has adherence been?" with only a vague memory to answer from.

What is a virtual caregiver dashboard?

A screen an adult child or caregiver logs into separately from the elderly person's own device, to remotely see and manage their medication status without being physically present. It would show:

  • Adherence status at a glance — "Mom took her 8am meds ✓" or "Dad missed his 2pm dose ⚠️"
  • Real-time missed-dose alerts — a push notification the moment a scheduled dose isn't confirmed
  • The full medication list — what they're on, dosages, schedule, refill status
  • Trend history — adherence rate over the past week/month, to catch a slipping pattern early
  • Multi-person management — one dashboard covering both aging parents if a caregiver looks after two
  • Remote actions — nudging a reminder, editing a medication, contacting them directly from the dashboard

Hero's $30–60/month hardware is largely selling this same caregiver-visibility outcome — the physical dispenser is just the data source (did the compartment open); the actual product a family pays for is the dashboard telling them their parent is on track without having to call and ask. Dosely already generates the equivalent signal in software via the photo+voice confirmation loop, so a dashboard built on that data delivers the same peace-of-mind outcome without the device cost.

What not to lead with

Don't market dosely as "a medicine reminder app" on its own. That's free everywhere — Amazon, CVS, Walgreens, and GoodRx all give it away to sell you something else. Every headline should pair the reminder with the AI-understanding or caregiver layer; "reminds you to take a pill" alone is a commodity, not a pitch.

Priority order

Refill + pharmacy referral first (consumer value and revenue in one feature, works in both markets), then family-plan pricing (infra already built), then the premium dashboard, then the AI assistant. RTM is pursued in parallel as a partnership conversation rather than code.

Medicare RTM billing — how much money, legally (US)

RTM is not selling data. It's providers billing Medicare for a monitoring service delivered through software like dosely. Dosely never touches Medicare — it licenses software to providers, who bill under RTM CPT codes:

  • 98975 — setup/patient education: ~$20 one-time
  • 98976/98977 — device/software supply with monitoring: ~$45–55/month (needs readings 16+ of every 30 days — dosely's daily dose confirmations easily satisfy this)
  • 98980/98981 — monthly treatment management: ~$50 first 20 min, ~$40 each additional

A provider bills roughly $100–150/patient/month fully utilized; dosely's cut as software vendor is typically $5–15/patient/month. Math: 1,000 enrolled patients ≈ $60–180K ARR; 10,000 ≈ $600K–1.8M ARR. Providers are motivated — they keep the $85–135/patient/month spread.

Legal requirements: the software must meet the FDA "medical device" definition (low-risk adherence software generally needs no clearance, but a compliance review is required); HIPAA BAAs with providers; and one honest caveat — current RTM device-supply codes are body-system-specific (respiratory 98976, musculoskeletal 98977, CBT 98978), with no dedicated general medication-adherence code yet, so the realistic path is partnering with an RTM aggregator (Hero's model via Assure Health) that has billing figured out.

When is it sellable? The RTM service is billable almost immediately — one signed home-health agency or RTM partner with consented patients makes the first dollar; 50 patients is a real pilot. The pharma data-licensing path (Medisafe's model) needs ~100K+ active users with per-condition cohorts before any pharma buyer will pay, plus HIPAA de-identification and upfront consent — years away, and optional given dosely's privacy positioning.
